## Why I label AI-drafted text

Publishing AI-drafted text as though a human wrote it is misleading. Actually it’s worse than that, it’s actively dishonest. It expects readers to spend their time and trust without knowing how the text was made. Sure, people can produce crap too but it’s different because it’s bounded by the rote effort of human attention. Since you cannot reliably tell without spending the time to read/analyze/scan it, the appropriate and responsible thing to do is label it.

I use AI because I write slowly. Without that help, some docs would stay out of date or never get written. But publishing sooner comes with a responsibility: make the text's origin visible and let readers decide what to do with that information.

**AI helps me publish docs sooner. The TextProv toggle reveals which text came from a model, down to the character.**

## What readers should know

1. **Readers cannot reliably infer origin from the text.** A fluent paragraph could have been written by a person or a model. If origin matters, it needs an explicit label. Character-level marking lets that label follow the text precisely.
2. **Review matters even when it leaves the wording unchanged.** When someone reviews a sentence, they check it against what they know and decide whether to stand behind it. An unreviewed sentence has not had that check. It may be excellent, but the reader cannot assume that someone has verified it.
3. **Reading takes time and trust.** When reviewed and unreviewed text look alike, readers may reasonably assume both have received the same care. Making ~~origin~~ source/voice and review status visible lets them decide how much trust to place in what they read.
4. **Labeling makes earlier publication an honest choice.** Waiting until every AI-assisted draft has been reviewed can leave gaps in the docs for months. Publishing without labels is dishonest. Publishing with clear labels lets readers judge whether the available draft is worth their attention.
5. **Readers should be free to use or ignore the highlighting.** Some will want to see every character that came from AI and read those passages more cautiously. Others will turn the highlighting off. Both choices are reasonable. The toggle provides information; the reader decides how to use it.
6. **Feedback helps decide what gets reviewed next.** Reports of errors, confusing explanations, or poor writing help direct review effort. They set priorities; they do not replace review.

## Why it matters

It matters for the same reason that we treat the words of a doctor as medical advice and the stuff that kids say as television content. It also matters in a similar way to how people make decisions about food. Is it organic, has it been treated respectfully, handled appropriately, were any animals involved in its production?

We’re only beginning to understand the health impacts of working with AI, to toil alongside it playing Slop or Not all day. It’s possible that there’s moral and emotional injury involved, in a way that a runner might bruise their heel or like tennis people elbow. There’s a lot to say on the topic but the point I want to make here is that there’s an energy differential. The cost of a machine leading you on is —figuratively speaking— close to zero. The cost for a human realizing they have wasted a day is not insignificant. If burnout is high effort and low/no progress on a goal over time, whether it’s a shitty coworker or machine as the source of those machinations—that strife—doesn’t matter much.
