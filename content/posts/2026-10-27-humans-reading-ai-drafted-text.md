## Why I label AI-drafted text

Up until 2022, it was safe to assume that even poorly written content was produced by a real human person. We didn't give it a second thought. Machine made text was templated, on the order or a few phrases and sentences, but for the most part was given away by the repetition . It was easy to tell the difference. Fast-forward to today, past a certain level of quality, it's not so easy to tell the difference between human vs AI produced text (once the obvious tells are removed). Statistical watermarking is a great start and works well for what it's designed to do: identifying blocks of text. But it's not readily visible to the reader and doesn't scale down to individual words and phrases. C2PA

When all writing was human writing, even when it's awful there was still some amount of human energy that went into it.

Passing off longform AI-drafted text as though a human wrote it is misleading. Actually it's more than that, it's dishonest.

Fast-forward to today, past a certain level of quality it's not (at least once it's past a certain quality level that doesn't include the Claude and ChatGPT tropes). When all writing is human writing, even when it's awful there is still an amount of human energy that went into it.

It expects readers to spend their time and energy without knowing how the text was made. Sure, people can produce crap too but it’s different because it’s bounded by the rote effort of human attention. Since you cannot reliably tell without spending the time to read/analyze/scan it, the appropriate and responsible thing to do is label it.

I enjoy using AI tools when I write because I'm a slow writer and a habitually non-linear thinker. Without the help, a lot of what I wanted to write would go stale and/or never see the light of day. With the help, I'm able to produce (mostly) decent quality drafts and get into a flow state more readily. But it comes with a cost: there's that much more content to review and edit! It just moves the bottleneck from writing to reviewing. So in order to actually Get It Done™, I need to publish sooner, even if the text is not fully reviewed.

And that's the dilemna I've been Sweating Bullets™ over these past few months. I do not want readers to pay the price for my shortcuts/life hacks/professional cheat codes/whatever you want to call them. I want to be honest about the origin of the text and whether it has been reviewed. I want readers to know what they are reading and how much trust they can place in it. I want to be able to publish sooner without misleading anyone.

## What readers should know

1. **Readers cannot reliably infer origin from the text.** A fluent paragraph could have been written by a person or a model. If origin matters, it needs an explicit label. Character-level marking lets that label follow the text precisely.
2. **Review matters even when it leaves the wording unchanged.** When someone reviews a sentence, they check it against what they know and decide whether to stand behind it. An unreviewed sentence has not had that check. It may be excellent, but the reader cannot assume that someone has verified it.
3. **Reading takes time and trust.** When reviewed and unreviewed text look alike, readers may reasonably assume both have received the same care. Making ~~origin~~ source/voice and review status visible lets them decide how much trust to place in what they read.
4. **Labeling makes earlier publication an honest choice.** Waiting until every AI-assisted draft has been reviewed can leave gaps in the docs for months. Publishing without labels is dishonest. Publishing with clear labels lets readers judge whether the available draft is worth their attention.
5. **Readers should be free to use or ignore the highlighting.** Some will want to see every character that came from AI and read those passages more cautiously. Others will turn the highlighting off. Both choices are reasonable. The toggle provides information; the reader decides how to use it.
6. **Feedback helps decide what gets reviewed next.** Reports of errors, confusing explanations, or poor writing help direct review effort. They set priorities; they do not replace review.

## Why it matters

It matters for the same reason that we treat the words of a doctor as medical advice and the stuff that kids say as television content. It also matters in a similar way to how people make decisions about food. Is it organic, has it been treated respectfully, handled appropriately, were any animals involved in its production?

We’re only beginning to understand the health impacts of working with AI, to toil alongside it playing Slop or Not all day. It’s possible that there’s moral and emotional injury involved, in a way that a runner might bruise their heel or like tennis people elbow. There’s a lot to say on the topic but the point I want to make here is that there’s an energy differential. The cost of a machine leading you on is —figuratively speaking— close to zero. The cost for a human realizing they have wasted a day is not insignificant. If burnout is high effort and low/no progress on a goal over time, whether it’s a shitty coworker or machine as the source of those machinations—that strife—doesn’t matter much.


**1. The claim.** One paragraph. I label AI-drafted text, and I say whether I've reviewed it. The label's job is to tell you how much human attention went into what you're about to read.

**2. The signal we lost.** Before 2022, every piece of text came with a guarantee nobody had to think about: someone spent at least the effort it took to write it. Even bad writing. That effort was a floor on care, and readers priced it in without noticing. AI drafting removes the floor, and the reader can't tell by looking.

**3. Why the floor mattered.** Reading still costs what it always did. Production now costs close to nothing. That asymmetry is the harm: a machine can lead you on for free, and you still lose the day. Burnout is effort without progress, and the source of the strife doesn't change the injury. It does change what can be done about it, because machine output can be labeled and a bad coworker can't. This is where the energy differential paragraph goes, and the only analogy you need is the one between writing cost and reading cost.

**4. Why I use it anyway.** The confession. Slow writer. Without AI, documentation goes stale or never ships. With it, drafts happen and flow state comes easier. The catch: the bottleneck moves from writing to reviewing, and to actually ship, I publish before review is done.

**5. The resolution.** I'm not willing to make readers pay for my shortcut. The label is how I publish sooner without misleading anyone. It discloses two things, origin and review status, and leaves the trust decision with the reader, where it belongs.

**6. What it looks like in practice.** The actual labels, what "reviewed" means, where they appear. If you don't have this section yet, it's the one readers will want most, because sections 1 through 5 are a promise and this is the delivery.
